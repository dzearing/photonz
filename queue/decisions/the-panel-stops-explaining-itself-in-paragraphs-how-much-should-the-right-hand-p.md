# How much the right hand panel should explain itself

## What happens today

Select three layers in a document and look at the panel on the right. Before you
reach a control you have to read past small grey prose, three separate
paragraphs, seven lines between them:

- Under Appearance: "3 layers. What you set here reaches every one of them, in
  one step, unless a row says underneath how many it reaches."
- Under Effects: "Nothing added yet. Use the plus above for a shadow, a glow, a
  border or a blur."
- Under Arrange: "3 layers line up with each other, not with the picture."

That is one selection. Other selections have their own. A copy of a component
was measured at six lines across four sections back on 2026-09-09.

Every one of these was written carefully and every one made sense on the day it
was added. Read once, they teach you something true. Read for the four hundredth
time they are furniture you scroll past.

## Why it is worth deciding rather than just building

Two reasons.

The first is that it cuts against a choice already made. On 2026-09-08 a task
called "Room says what it will do" deliberately put two sentences of this kind
BACK into the panel, because a control that was silent about what it would do
was confusing people. Quietly taking them out again a few days later, on a
manager's judgment, is not a runner's call to make.

The second is that the room is contested and the amount is known. A previous
investigation measured the panel's sections at 1,090 points of content trying to
fit a dock 688 points tall. Three scripted walks fail today for exactly this
reason: the control they are reaching for has been scrolled out of the window.
So this is not only about taste. Seven lines of prose is roughly one more
control's worth of height on a panel that is already about four hundred points
short.

What is NOT being asked here: which section comes first. That is a separate
question, already on the board. This one is about how much each section is
allowed to say once it gets there.

## The four answers

**One short line, and only when it earns it.** A section may say one short
sentence, and only when it is telling you something you could not have worked
out by looking at what is on screen. "3 layers line up with each other, not with
the picture" survives, because nothing else says it. "Use the plus above for a
shadow, a glow, a border or a blur" does not, because the plus is right there
and says so when you open it. On a three layer selection, seven lines becomes
about two. The risk is losing the one sentence somebody was leaning on, and the
answer to that is that anything cut which was the only statement of a limit or a
scope moves into that control's own tooltip rather than vanishing.

**Behind a small question mark.** Nothing grey by default. A section with
something to explain grows a small question mark beside its heading, and the
sentence appears when you click it. This buys the most room of any answer and
throws nothing away, but an explanation nobody can see is an explanation nobody
finds, which is the same as not having written it. It also adds one more small
control to every section heading in a panel that is already dense. Softened by
showing the sentence automatically the first few times a section is opened.

**Say it while it is new, then stop.** The panel explains itself the first
handful of times you meet a section and then goes quiet for good. A new person
gets the words exactly when they help; everybody else gets the room back without
having to ask. The cost is that the panel is then a different shape on two
machines, which makes it harder to talk about, screenshot, test or support, and
somebody returning after a month has lost the sentence with no way back short of
a settings switch.

**Leave them as they are.** Nothing changes. The sentences stay, the room
problem gets solved by fixing the dock rather than by taking words away, and
nothing that was deliberately added gets reversed. Honest, and it is the safest
answer for anybody currently relying on one of these lines. The cost is that the
room problem is real today while the dock work is two stages away, and that
nothing stops the next section adding its own paragraph. Choosing this retires
the task.

## Recommended

**One short line, and only when it earns it.** It is the only answer that fixes
the problem today without either hiding the useful sentences or making the panel
behave differently on different machines, and it leaves a rule behind that the
next section can be held to.
