Animating an icon and editing a video

· study · are they the same job?

Sixteen clickthroughs now exist for these two jobs: six for animating an icon and ten for editing a video. This page puts them beside each other, job for job, and says where they are genuinely one experience and where they only look like one. The short answer: they share four things, and the fourth one is the surprise, because it is the timing surface itself. What they still cannot share is the thing most people mean when they say "one timeline": the ruler. An icon repeats and a video finishes, and that one fact still drives every remaining difference between them. Every claim below is shown rather than argued: each cell names the page and the step it came from, and you can open it.

Revised 15 September 2026. The first version of this page compared a set of animation mocks that offered a short menu of canned motions, Pulse and Spin and Draw on, and it concluded that icon timing was one typed number and that a list of parts beat a track. That premise was rejected: you animate a property of a layer, the way you add an effect to one, and the timing needs a surface with real width. The mocks were rebuilt on that model as Make a bell swing and Make the knob lag the bell, and this page is rewritten against them. The rebuild moved three rows of the table towards video and left the crux exactly where it was.

Genuinely the same control

Motion is a list on the selected layer, built out of the row an effect already uses. The curve is a named shape you can also draw. The timing surface is one component with tracks, bars and a playhead. And a hand-off sheet that asks where the file is going before it asks for a format.

Surfaces that only look shared

The ruler itself, how long something takes, what a shape turns around, how you judge whether it is any good, and what actually comes out at the end. Each one looks like a small difference and is not.

Things only one of them has

What the table is saying now. The two jobs agree about what a motion is: a property of a layer, changing from one value to another, over a stretch of time, on a curve, shown as a bar in a lane. They disagree about what the time is. Everything still marked different comes from that one disagreement, and the rebuild did not soften it: a bar that runs past the end of the cycle, which is the whole point of the lag on the bell, is a sentence an absolute ruler cannot say.

The crux

The ruler is the thing they cannot share

One component, two measures, and only one of them has an end
An icon: one cycle, and then it happens again
and again
0450900 ms · repeatsforever

The ruler is one cycle, not a document. It reads in milliseconds because that is what a person setting a swing thinks in, but what it measures is a lap: at the dashed line the same motion starts over, and the value there is the value it started at. That is not a convention, it is the only thing a repeating animation must get right, and it is what the app can check for you. On the way out these become percentages of a cycle, which is literally what a CSS keyframe block and a SMIL keyTimes list are.

A video: absolute seconds, and a last frame
0:000:080:18 · last frame

A key is at 0:04 and it means 0:04. After the last key the value holds to the end of the document, and the red cap on the right is that end. Every video flow leans on it: a trim moves an out point, a transition is paid for with spare frames either side of a cut, a freeze inserts three seconds and the document grows to 0:21.

These cannot be one ruler, and the rebuild made the case sharper rather than weaker. The clearest proof is now a single step: the knob's bar runs past the line where the cycle restarts, because the knob is still finishing its swing while the bell has already begun the next one. That is not a mistake to tidy away, it is what a lag is in something that loops, and there is no way to draw it on a ruler whose grammar is "this is where it stops". Underneath, three of the four icon scenarios have no end in them at all: a spinner is one turn plus the word forever, a notification pulse runs while there is something new, and a toggle is two drawings and a crossing with no duration to sit on until somebody taps it. Stretching a document ruler over those produces a document that is nine hundred milliseconds long for no reason anybody can explain.

The open case

What proved the strip, and what is still unproved

Two parts, shown working. Six parts, still drawn rather than walked

The first round of mocks stopped at one moving part, and at one part a column of fields is plainly enough. Two parts is where that stops being true, and two parts is now walked end to end: the bell swings, the knob carries the same motion ninety milliseconds later, and the only way to set that is to drag one bar against another and read the gap. A relationship cannot be read in a column of numbers. Beyond two, the question is whether anything changes again, so here is the case nobody has walked: six strokes of a wordmark drawing themselves on in order, each leaning into the one before it.

The overlap is the answer to the question, and you get it without reading a number. This is unmistakably timeline-shaped, and it is still not the video timeline: the axis is one cycle, there is no right-hand end, there are no clips, no media, no audio and nothing to trim. It is the same strip the bell and the knob sit on, with four more lanes in it.

The honest reading, and what changed in it. A whole icon SET does not animate together, because icons animate on their own triggers in whatever app they end up in, so there is still no case for a shared ruler across a library. What does happen is ONE icon with several parts, and the number of parts where a list stops working turned out to be two, not four. That is why the strip is no longer a "later, on evidence" item: the evidence is the flagship flow. What is still unproved is everything past two parts, in particular whether six lanes want grouping, and that is a question for the first person who animates a wordmark, not a reason to wait.

What video would have to become. Video in Photonz today is a separate editor, not a document. It is its own window with its own state, it holds one clip, and what it can do to that clip is trim it, crop it and export it: the whole stored model is an in point, an out point and a crop rectangle. The layer document has no notion of time at all, and there is no keyframe anywhere in the engine. So "share a timeline with video" does not mean connecting two things that exist. It means first rewriting video into a multi-clip, multi-track document with audio, a playhead in the render path and a compositor that samples media per frame, and only then having something to share. That is the whole video feature, built before an icon can swing, and the icon side gets nothing from it: no part of codecs, audio or frame scheduling teaches you how to emit a keyframe block as text.

The asymmetry, stated plainly. Doing animation first genuinely helps video later: video inherits a settled curve vocabulary, a tested idea of a motion as an entry on a property, and the timing strip itself, tracks, bars, playhead and all, which is now the same component either job would use. Doing video first helps animation not at all. That is not a surprise and this page is not presenting it as one; it is the expected answer, and the evidence above is what makes it safe to act on rather than assume.

And there is a second consolidation, still worth more than this one. The toggle flow's States group, a named list of states, a count of how many things differ between them, and one duration for the crossing, is structurally the same surface as component variants on the UI side. Those two are both live work right now, unlike video, and if they are built separately they will drift the way the curve controls drifted. Compare icon-states-wt with ui-variants and the overlap is closer than anything on this page.

Recommendation

What to build first

Five slices, in this order. Each one is judgeable on its own.
A property of a layer can change over one cycle. Motion is a list in Properties with a plus that offers this layer's own properties. One entry carries from, to, start, over, curve and repeat, and the icon plays in the canvas. Nothing else in this list works until this does.
Rotation says what it turns around. A pivot you drag on the canvas, there from the moment a rotation exists. Without it the flagship motion is a bobblehead, and that is not a polish item: it is the difference between a swinging bell and a broken one.
The timing strip across the bottom. One cycle, a lane per animated property, a bar you drag, a dashed line where it repeats, and a bar allowed to cross that line. This is what makes a second moving part possible, and it is the surface video would inherit unchanged.
The loop is the review. Play it at 16, 24, 32 and 48 points at once with a speed control, because ninety milliseconds is under six frames. This replaces scrubbing, and without it nobody can tell whether the motion is any good.
It leaves as an animated SVG. The motion exports as text alongside the static SVG export already planned, and the sheet says in plain words what survives the trip and what does not.

What this does not include, on purpose. Video does not move. It stays the separate editor it is today, and it inherits the curve list and the timing strip when it is its turn, by using the same components rather than by being rewritten first. And there is no project type picker: the two jobs already live in separate windows, so nothing needs hiding from anything, and the surface can keep following the document, which is a document with a duration getting a transport and a document with a repeating motion getting a cycle strip.
