Animating an icon and editing a video

· study · are they the same job?

Fifteen clickthroughs now exist for these two jobs: five for animating an icon and ten for editing a video. This page puts them beside each other, step for step, and says where they are genuinely one experience and where they only look like one. The short answer: they share three things and nothing else, the three they share are small and worth building once, and the one thing everybody assumes they share, a ruler you scrub a playhead along, is the thing they cannot share at all. Every claim below is shown rather than argued: each cell names the page and the step it came from, and you can open it.

3

Genuinely the same control

Select a layer and its motion is a section in Properties. Easing named on a segment. An export sheet that asks where the file is going before it asks for a format, which is the sheet rather than the export: what comes out of it could hardly be more different.

5

Surfaces that look shared

Five of them, and not one survives a close look: the ruler, the front door to animating anything, the preview, the way several moving things get ordered, and the export itself.

13

Things only one of them has

Seven things only video needs and six only icons need. None of them is a rounding error: each one is a whole surface.

What the table is really saying. The two flows agree at the ends and disagree in the middle. Picking the thing and shaping the curve are identical. Everything between them, how long, whether it ends, how several things are ordered, and how you judge it, is different, and it is different because of one fact: an icon animation repeats and a video finishes.

The crux

The ruler is the thing they cannot share

Same shape on screen, two different measures, and only one of them has an end
An icon: one cycle, in percent, no end
and again
0%62%100% = 0%forever

The last key and the first key are the same key. That is not a convention, it is the only thing a repeating animation must get right, and it is what the app can check for you. There is no absolute time anywhere on this strip, which is exactly what a CSS keyframe block and a SMIL keyTimes list are: percentages of a cycle. Changing "one pass takes 0.9s" stretches the whole strip and every key keeps its place.

A video: absolute seconds, and a last frame
0:000:080:18 · last frame

A key is at 0:04 and it means 0:04. After the last key the value holds to the end of the document, and the red cap on the right is that end. Every video flow leans on it: a trim moves an out point, a transition is paid for with spare frames either side of a cut, a freeze inserts three seconds and the document grows to 0:21.

These cannot be one ruler, and the reason is not technical. Three of the four icon scenarios have no end in them at all. A spinner is one turn plus the word forever. A notification pulse runs while there is something new. A toggle is two drawings and a crossing between them, with no duration to sit on until you tap it. A ruler whose whole grammar is "this is where it stops" cannot draw any of those, and stretching one to try produces a document that is four seconds long for no reason anybody can explain. Only the draw-on badge has an order in it, and that is the case the next block is about.

The open case

The case that decides whether a strip earns its place

A six-part logo drawing itself on, in the two forms it could take

The five animation flows all stop at two moving parts, and at two parts a numbered list is plainly enough. Nothing showed a whole set moving together, and that omission is doing work: it is where a list would stop being readable and something time-shaped would start to earn its keep. So here is that case. Six strokes of a wordmark draw themselves on in order, each leaning into the one before it. Same six parts, same six numbers, drawn twice.

The overlap is the answer to the question, and you get it without reading a number. This is unmistakably timeline-shaped, and it is still not the video timeline: the axis is 0 to 100% of one cycle, there is no right-hand end, there are no clips, no media, no audio and nothing to trim. It is a picture of the same six numbers in the list beside it.

The honest reading. A whole icon SET does not animate together, because icons animate on their own triggers in whatever app they end up in, so there is no case for a shared ruler across a library. What does happen is ONE icon with several parts, and the list holds up to about three of them and stops. So the lanes are worth building, and they are worth building second: the list is what the first four scenarios need, the lanes are a view of the same numbers, and adding the view later changes nothing above it. Building the lanes first would mean drawing a timeline before a single icon can pulse, which is the order this study exists to avoid.

What video would have to become. Video in Photonz today is a separate editor, not a document. It is its own window with its own state, it holds one clip, and what it can do to that clip is trim it, crop it and export it: the whole stored model is an in point, an out point and a crop rectangle. The layer document has no notion of time at all, and there is no keyframe anywhere in the engine. So "share a timeline with video" does not mean connecting two things that exist. It means first rewriting video into a multi-clip, multi-track document with audio, a playhead in the render path and a compositor that samples media per frame, and only then having something to share. That is the whole video feature, built before an icon can pulse, and the icon side gets nothing from it: no part of codecs, audio or frame scheduling teaches you how to emit a SMIL keyframe block.

The asymmetry, stated plainly. Doing animation first genuinely helps video later: video inherits a settled easing vocabulary, a tested idea of a key as a value on a property at a moment, and a hand-off sheet that asks about the destination. Doing video first helps animation not at all. That is not a surprise and this page is not presenting it as one; it is the expected answer, and the evidence above is what makes it safe to act on rather than assume.

And the consolidation actually worth having is not this one. The toggle flow's States group, a named list of states, a count of how many things differ between them, and one duration for the crossing, is structurally the same surface as component variants on the UI side. Those two are both live work right now, unlike video, and if they are built separately they will drift exactly the way the four easing vocabularies above drifted. Compare icon-states-wt with ui-variants and the overlap is closer than anything on this page.

Recommendation

What to build first

Four slices. The first three are the feature; the fourth waits for evidence.
Motion is a property of a layer. Select a glyph, pick a named motion from a short list, and set how much, how long one pass takes, which curve, whether it repeats, and what it turns around. No playhead, no ruler, no tracks. This is the whole of the pulse flow and the whole of the spinner flow.
The preview is the review. The icon preview strip that already exists in the app runs the motion, at every size at once, with a speed control so you can slow it to a quarter and actually see it. This is what replaces scrubbing, and without it nobody can tell whether the motion is any good.
It leaves as an animated SVG, and the sheet asks where it is going. The motion exports as text alongside the static export that is already planned, and the sheet says in plain words what survives the trip.
Later, and only on evidence: the cycle strip. One horizontal strip, 0 to 100% of a cycle, a lane per animated part. It is a second view of numbers that already exist, so it changes nothing above it, and it earns its place the first time somebody animates four parts of one icon and cannot read the list.

What this does not include, on purpose. No project type picker. The two jobs already live in separate windows, so nothing needs hiding from anything, and the surface can keep following the document the way it does today: a document with a duration gets a transport, a document with a repeating motion gets a cycle strip, and neither has to ask the person which kind of thing they are making before they start.
