Animate a toggle between two states

· usage clickthrough · one scenario

One job: the bookmark should fill in when you save something, and empty out when you unsave it. This is the flow where the animation is not authored, it is derived. You say what the icon looks like when it is off and what it looks like when it is on, and the difference between those two drawings writes the motion entry for you. So the canvas holds two artboards, neither of which ever moves, and the crossing is shown in the preview where it belongs. It uses the same Motion list and the same timing dock as every other animation flow, and the dock is 320 milliseconds wide, the shortest in the set, because that is how long a toggle actually lasts. It is also the flow with the biggest hole in it, because an SVG on its own cannot feel a tap, and the page says so rather than drawing a control that would have to be invented. Seven real clicks on ONE screen, which never re-renders below.

The app has no answer for any of this yet. A layer has no states, there is no transition between two drawings of the same thing, no timing surface of any kind, and nothing exports SVG. The nearest thing that does exist is component variants, which is the argument this page is really making: icon states and component states are the same idea and should not be built twice.

  1. 1

    the Bookmark layer

    the Layers group, at the top of the dock

    Properties shows this glyph’s Appearance, and the header above it says in Off. There is already exactly one state and it is the drawing you have: nothing has been added, the panel is just naming what was always true. Below Properties sit the same two lists every other glyph has, Effects and Motion, and Motion is empty because one drawing cannot differ from itself.

  2. 2

    the + on the States group, and name it On

    the States group header

    a second artboard appears beside the first, identical to it, and the panel now lists two states. The new one starts as a copy on purpose, so it can only ever differ where you make it differ. Motion is still empty, and the strip below still says none, because two identical drawings have nothing to cross.

  3. 3

    the Fill row, and choose Same as stroke

    Properties, under Appearance

    the right-hand artboard fills in and the left one does not, because you were standing in On when you changed it. Then look at the Motion list: an entry appeared and you did not add it. It is marked from states, and a bar for it has landed on the strip. This is the page’s whole argument: on a toggle you do not author the animation, you author the two ends and the difference between them is the animation.

  4. 4

    220 in the Over field

    Motion, under the Fill entry

    the number lands on the arrow between the two artboards and the preview starts crossing back and forth at that rate. It is the same Over field as every other animation page, holding the one number this flow has. The two artboards have not moved and never will: they are the drawings, and only the preview animates, which is what keeps them editable while the crossing is running.

  5. 5

    the plus on Motion, then Scale, and set To to 114

    the Motion group header

    the bookmark now swells slightly past full size and settles, which is the difference between a toggle that acknowledges you and one that merely changes colour. This entry is the opposite of the last one: no difference between the two drawings could have produced it, so it is the one you add. And now look at the strip, because this is the part a column could not have told you: the two bars are not the same length. The colour is finished at 220 and the swell is still settling at 320, so the crossing is 0.32 seconds, not the 0.22 you typed.

  6. 6

    the Trigger row, and choose On tap

    Properties, under Happens on

    This is the hole, and the app says so instead of pretending. A picture dropped into an image tag has no events, so a tap-driven toggle can only be handed over as two states plus a class for the host to swap. Photonz can hand over the crossing, both bars of it; it cannot hand over the tap. Saying that here beats letting somebody discover it after the export.

  7. 7

    at the 16 copy, crossing back and forth

    the Real size card

    the two artboards never moved, and they should not: they are the drawings. Only the preview animates, which is what keeps the states editable while the crossing is running. At 16 the fill carries the whole message and the swell is barely visible, which is worth knowing before you spend any more of the crossing on it: the longer of your two bars is the one doing the least work at the size this icon will be used.

The idea

A toggle is two drawings and a crossing. You never lay the motion out: make the drawings differ and the entry writes itself, marked from states so you can see it was not yours. The artboards are the states and they never animate; the preview is the only thing that moves.

The consolidation worth having

This is component variants wearing an icon's clothes. The same list, the same "differs in this state" count, the same crossing. If the app builds icon states and component states separately it will have built the same feature twice and they will drift. See .

What is missing, plainly

The trigger. An SVG in an image tag has no events, so the tap belongs to whoever receives the file. The app can export the two states and the crossing; it cannot export the tap, and any mock that draws a working On tap control is lying.

What the dock is earning here

One fact, and it is one no column could have held: the two bars are different lengths. The colour is done at 220 milliseconds and the swell is still settling at 320, so the crossing is longer than the field that names it. When overshoot was a bare percentage nothing on screen said that, and a swell that is out of step with the change it decorates is exactly what a twitchy toggle is.

Open question

Should a crossing be one number for both directions, as drawn, or two? On is usually meant to feel quicker than off. Two numbers is more truthful and it doubles the controls on a surface whose whole appeal is that it has almost none.
