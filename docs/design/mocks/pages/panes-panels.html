Panes & panels

· surfaces & primitives App design

Two words that get used interchangeably everywhere else, and must not be here. A panel is the container: one column on the right, one width, one collapse state. A pane is a section inside it: Layers, Properties, Effects, Library. You resize panes against each other inside a panel, and you resize the panel against the canvas. Those are different gestures with different limits, and once you can name them the shell stops needing new chrome as the app grows: new capability is a new pane, never a new window.

01

Anatomy

One container, many sections. Everything in the shell that is not the canvas, the tool bar or an overlay is a pane in the one panel. Try it: drag either divider, then click a pane header. The panel keeps its width through both, and no pane changes position in the stack. That containment is the whole point of the split.

Say "the Layers pane, in the panel". Say "the Layers panel" and leave the container unnamed.

The test for which word you want: ask what the thing argues with. If it argues with the canvas for width, it is the panel. If it argues with its neighbours for height, it is a pane. Nothing in the shell argues with both.

The growing pane is the LAST one, and that is a rule, not a habit. Whichever pane absorbs the leftover space also absorbs whatever a fold or a hide gives back, so it decides where everything else ends up. Put it last and folding a pane leaves every pane above it exactly where it was. Put it first and folding Properties makes Layers swallow the gap and shove the folded header to the bottom of the panel, which reads as the pane having been moved rather than closed. That is why Library sits at the bottom: it is the browse surface, so it is the one that can afford to change size.

The corollary: Layers is never the growing pane. It gets a bounded budget and its own scroller, so a sixty-layer document scrolls inside it, the divider under it decides how much of the panel it consumes, and it keeps scrolling at every size you drag it to.

Nobody picks a pixel height. Every page used to hand-tune one, so the same pane opened to a different size nearly everywhere: measured across seventeen windows, Layers had eleven distinct heights and Properties thirteen. A pane now declares its role and the ladder gives it one of three budgets. Exactly one pane per panel may grow; everything else is bounded, which is what keeps the stack predictable when you collapse something. If the content overruns its budget the pane scrolls. That is what the budget is for.

04

The floor is real

A limit you can feel is worth more than a limit you are told about. At the floor the divider simply stops: no rubber-band, no snap-back, no shake. The pointer keeps moving and the divider does not, which is the clearest way to say "that is as far as this goes".

Stop dead at min and max. Bounce, or squeeze a neighbour below its own floor.

API pane("layers").height = 96

05

The divider, in three states

Idle · hover and drag · at the limit
Idle · grip visible

A 3px grip at rest, so you can see the seam is draggable without hunting for it. The hit target is 8px, wider than the grip you can see.

Hover and drag · accent

The whole seam lights up and the cursor becomes a resize cursor. Hover and drag paint identically, because a drag that looks different from its own hover reads as a mode you entered.

At the limit · it stops

No new colour, no warning, no motion. The absence of movement is the feedback, and it needs no vocabulary of its own.

06

Resizing panes in a panel

Zero sum, two neighbours, never the window. Drag either seam and watch the panel width and the pane order stay exactly where they were. The panel is a fixed budget being divided, which is why a pane can never make the window bigger and a long list can never push a sibling off the screen.

Take the height from the growing pane. Grow the panel, or push a pane out of the visible stack.

Level 3 · the panel becomes a rail

"The canvas is the subject." The panel becomes a 34px labeled strip. Clicking a tab restores the panel and reveals that pane, so one click gets you back to what you wanted.

One rule ties the three together: a restore lands you where you were. Un-folding a pane returns the exact height you dragged it to, not the role default. Un-hiding puts it back in its fixed slot, never at the bottom of the stack. Restoring the panel returns your dragged width. Collapse is a view, not an edit, and a view that forgets is indistinguishable from one that broke.

What movesDurationWhy
Splitter drag · pane height, panel width0msTracks the pointer one to one. Any easing at all makes the divider trail your hand, which reads as the app being slow rather than smooth. A transition here is a bug, not polish.

What the window did · one attribute, three states
1The panel left the layout. It floats over the canvas with its own shadow, so a 400px window still gets a full canvas and a full panel, just not both at once.
2It gained a close affordance. A floating surface has to be dismissible from itself; a docked one does not, because its rail is always there.
3The rail lost its labels below 620px and kept its icons and its tooltips, so the tabs stay reachable rather than being dropped.
4Panes reflowed internally. The property grid went from two columns to one. That is a decision inside a pane, not a change to the panel.
5One attribute drives all of it — open, closed, overlay — so the narrow layout cannot drift away from the wide one as either changes.
At the panelInside a paneNever
Below an 880px window the panel leaves the layout and becomes an overlay you summon. Below 620px the rail drops its labels to icons. Both are queries on the window, not the screen, so a small window on a large display behaves the same way. Two-column property grids reflow to one. A scope switch becomes a wrapping chip list rather than a segmented control, because segments do not survive a 268px column. A search field and a button stack instead of sharing one skinny row. The pane order, the pane names, the collapse state, or which panes exist. A narrow window is a smaller view of the same panel, not a different app, and never a place to quietly drop a feature.
10

Rules

Do and don't
Add capability as a new pane in the one panel. Add a second panel, a left dock, or a floating window. Give a pane a role and let the ladder size it. Write a pixel height because it looks right on your page. Let a long list scroll inside its own pane. Let a pane grow the panel, or the panel grow the window. Put the growing pane last, and give the rest a budget. Let a pane above swallow the gap and push a folded header down. Animate folding, hiding, and the rail transition. Animate anything a pointer is currently dragging. Keep every pane header clickable, with a visible chevron. Ship a pane you cannot fold or a panel you cannot manage. Restore a pane to the size and the slot it had. Reset it to the default, or re-add it at the bottom.

In the API

Every drag and click is a value an agent can set
CallWhat it does
panel.width = 320Resize the panel against the canvas. Clamped to 180 to 460, exactly like the drag.
panel.collapsed = trueCollapse the whole panel to the rail. ⌥⌘L and the rail tabs call the same thing.
panel.reveal("layers")Restore the panel if it was railed, un-hide and unfold that pane, and scroll it into view. What a rail tab does.
pane("properties").height = 240Set one pane's height. Clamped to its own min and max; the growing pane absorbs the difference.
pane("effects").collapsed = trueFold a pane to its header. The reversible one.
pane("library").visible = falseRemove the pane from the panel entirely. What the Panels footer checklist toggles.
pane("layers").reset()Back to the role budget. What a double-click on the divider does.
panel.layoutThe whole arrangement as one value: width, order, per-pane height, folded and visible. Save it, name it, restore it, hand it to someone else.

Because the layout is one readable value, a workspace preset is not a feature anyone has to build. "Give me a layout for redlining" is an agent reading panel.layout, writing a new one, and the panel animating into it, using the same calls the mouse makes.

The idea

Naming the two boxes is what stops the shell from sprawling. A panel is the only thing that argues with the canvas; a pane is the only thing that argues with its siblings. Every new capability then has an obvious home and an obvious size, so the app can grow into UI design, components and video without ever needing chrome nobody has seen before.

A direction

Let a pane be pulled out of the panel and parked on a second display, still driven by the same selection. That is the one arrangement a single column genuinely cannot serve, and it stays honest as long as the pane keeps its identity and comes home to its original slot.

Open question

Should the panel layout follow the workspace, the document, or the person? Following the workspace makes Video feel purpose-built. Following the person means your panel never moves under you. Today it follows the person, and the workspace only chooses which panes are visible.
